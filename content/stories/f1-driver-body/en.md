## Few numbers, and they differ

Writing about an F1 driver's body tends to come with plenty of adjectives and not much hard data. In 2026 the British Journal of Sports Medicine published a review of all the available research and combined it with interviews with three F1 driver performance coaches. The authors' conclusion: data are scarce, because most of the knowledge stays inside the teams. Drivers are not especially exceptional in aerobic fitness, stature or body mass, but their necks are noticeably stronger. And many preparation methods rest on experience rather than measurements.[^c1]

So what follows are typical values with a source and a year. Where the sources disagree, we give both versions.

## Braking: 4–6 g

F1.com says that under heavy braking a driver experiences up to 6 g, a load six times their body weight. Aston Martin also speaks of more than 6 g under braking and in some fast corners. Brembo, whose engineers work with all the teams, gives lower peaks from its own simulations of the 2025 season: 4.7 g at Turn 1 in Monza and 4.9 g at Turn 7 in Singapore.[^c2] The driver presses the pedal with a force of around 160 kg: F1.com gives the same figure, and Brembo quotes 162 kg for that same Monza corner.[^c3]

In 2026 the numbers fell. The new cars brake for longer and less violently. At Turn 18 at Spa, Brembo calculated 3.3 g against 4.7 g a year earlier, and the pedal load dropped from 160 to 96 kg. At Turn 10 in Barcelona the deceleration fell from 4 to 3.3 g.[^c4]

## Corners: the neck never rests

Sources also differ on lateral loads. According to Mercedes, the car generates more than 5 g at Turn 1 in Suzuka, the highest lateral load on the 2024 calendar. Aston Martin writes of more than 6 g in some fast corners.[^c5] Former driver Pedro de la Rosa, now an Aston Martin ambassador, believes corners take the most out of the body:

> "Sustained lateral g-force from cornering hundreds of times over a race distance is the toughest force on your body when driving."[^c6]

The head and helmet together weigh around 7 kg, and under such loads they can feel like 30–40 kg. That is how Aston Martin team doctor Rahul Chotai explains it.[^c7] So the neck gets trained almost every day: according to his trainer John Malvern, Lando Norris does neck work at least five times a week.[^c8] Drivers lift up to 40 kg with their necks and train in weighted helmets, and Mercedes shows an exercise in which 30 kg is strapped to the head and held while the head hangs off the edge of a table.[^c9]

Antti Kontsas, Sebastian Vettel's former coach, explains what it is all for:

> "The real reason why the neck is so important is that you need to see where you're going. It's all about precision."[^c10]

Without constant training, the neck gives in quickly. De la Rosa recalls that before testing the Aston Martin AMR23 at Silverstone, his first time in an F1 car for around a decade, he trained his neck as much as he could. Even so, after a few laps through the right-handers at Luffield, Woodcote and Copse he had to rest his head on the headrest, and the next morning his neck was very stiff.[^c11]

## The heart: marathon levels

During a race the heart rate can stay above 170 beats per minute for long periods. According to Aston Martin, it generally ranges from 150 to 190, and de la Rosa calls around 170 typical for a race.[^c12] At the start, he says, it often goes past 180, and his own record, 204 beats, came back in Formula 3.[^c13]

## Heat: a few kilograms lighter

The cockpit can reach 50–60°C, and more than 60°C in Singapore.[^c14] Over a race a driver typically loses two to three litres of sweat and finishes 2–3% dehydrated. According to Chotai, even 2% dehydration impairs reaction time, concentration and decision-making.[^c15]

F1.com calls Singapore the toughest race of the season: it almost always runs close to two hours.[^c19] Accounts of how much weight drivers lose there vary. Pierre Gasly spoke of 2.5 kg in 2022. George Russell said 4 kg after the 2024 race, and Nico Hülkenberg said 3 kg the same evening. RacingNews365 writes that losing around 5 kg there is not uncommon.[^c16] De la Rosa's hardest race came in Malaysia in 2002. His drinks system failed, he was close to fainting at the finish, and he lost 3.5 kg.[^c17]

There is not much to drink in the car: a 1–1.5-litre pouch from which a pump sends fluid into the helmet when the driver presses a button on the wheel.[^c18]

## Qatar 2023: the limit

The Qatar Grand Prix on 8 October 2023 showed where the limit lies even for a very well-trained body. Logan Sargeant retired after 40 laps because he felt unwell. Esteban Ocon was sick in his helmet. Alexander Albon was checked at the medical centre after the finish, and Lance Stroll said he had been passing out on track.[^c20] According to Russell, it was over 50°C in the cockpit.[^c21] After the race Lando Norris said:

> "I think today we probably found the limit."[^c22]

The next day the FIA acknowledged that the heat and humidity had affected the drivers' wellbeing and promised to look at changes: guidance for teams, better cockpit airflow, adjustments to the calendar.[^c23] The statement also said:

> "While being elite athletes, they should not be expected to compete under conditions that could jeopardise their health or safety."[^c23]

The 2024 race was already scheduled later in the year, when Qatar is cooler.[^c24] From 2025 a "heat hazard" rule came in. When the air temperature measured by the FIA's sensors at the track reaches 31°C, the car's minimum weight rises by 5 kg so teams can fit a cooling system: a shirt through which chilled fluid flows along 48 metres of tubing.[^c25] At the 2025 Singapore Grand Prix the FIA declared a heat hazard, and teams had to fit the system to their cars.[^c26]

## Reactions: 0.2 seconds

McLaren writes that an ordinary person reacts in 0.15–0.2 seconds. But at the start a driver has to do more than notice the lights going out: they also have to release the clutch correctly. Including that action, a driver's reaction time is usually 0.2–0.3 seconds. Aston Martin gives an average of 0.16 seconds at the start for Fernando Alonso.[^c27] The figures differ because each measures in its own way.

## Driver weight: an 80 kg rule, now 82 kg

Until 2019 the driver was weighed together with the car, and the combined minimum in 2018 was 734 kg. Heavier drivers lost out to lighter ones, who had more ballast left to tune the car's balance. From 2019 the driver with seat and equipment has to weigh at least 80 kg, with any shortfall made up by ballast.[^c28] There was also a medical reason: in the V8 era teams urged heavier drivers to slim down, raising fears for their health. The new minimum was meant to spare drivers the need for drastic dieting.[^c29]

The ballast goes next to the seat, within the cockpit, so a lighter driver can no longer spread it around the car for better balance.[^c31] From 2025 the minimum went up to 82 kg, and the 2026 regulations kept that figure.[^c30]
