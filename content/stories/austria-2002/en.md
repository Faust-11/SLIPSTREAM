## Eight laps from home

On 12 May 2002 the A1-Ring at Spielberg hosted the sixth round of the world championship.[^c1] Michael Schumacher arrived in Austria as the clear favourite: he had won four of the season's five races.[^c2] But this weekend belonged to his team-mate. Rubens Barrichello took pole and led almost the entire race.[^c3]

Both Ferraris were on two-stop strategies. On lap 61 Barrichello pitted, Schumacher led for a single lap, then stopped on the next lap himself, and the Brazilian was back in front.[^c4] According to Ferrari sporting director Jean Todt, that was when, on lap 63 of 71, the drivers were told to swap places.[^c5]

Schumacher, meanwhile, kept pushing: in the closing laps it was he who set the race's fastest lap, chipping away at his team-mate's lead.[^c34] Nobody behind the two Ferraris was a threat; the Williams cars of Montoya and Ralf Schumacher finished more than 17 seconds back.[^c33]

Barrichello started the final lap about a second ahead. Out of the last corner he braked sharply, and Schumacher slipped past just before the line. The gap at the flag was 0.182 seconds.[^c6]

[[media:m2]]

The grandstands answered with jeers.[^c7]

## An upside-down podium

What came next angered more than just the spectators. Schumacher ushered Barrichello onto the top step of the podium, so the Brazilian stood there while the German anthem played. For the Italian anthem they stood there together. Schumacher took the winner's trophy from Austrian Chancellor Wolfgang Schüssel without acknowledging him, and promptly handed it to his team-mate.[^c8]

[[media:m3]]

Both drivers looked as if they had been caught out. "I was asked to move over. It was a team decision and I have to respect it," said Barrichello.[^c9] Schumacher kept his distance too: "But obviously I don't take a lot of joy from the victory."[^c10] He added that before the race, when asked about team tactics, he had said: "I don't believe that there is going to be a team strategy involved." And then, as he put it, he was suddenly told that Rubens would move over.[^c35]

Todt was not about to apologise. "I am not really bothered. Sometimes difficult decisions have to be taken," he said when asked about the crowd's reaction.[^c11] His main argument: in 1997, 1998 and 1999 Ferrari had lost the championship at the final race of the season.[^c12] Technical director Ross Brawn admitted the swap could have been hidden behind pit-stop tactics, but doing it covertly, he said, "would have taken credit away from Rubens".[^c13]

What was at stake was four points: ten for the win instead of six for second.[^c14] After Austria Schumacher had 54 points, twice as many as his nearest pursuer, Juan Pablo Montoya, on 27. Barrichello was fourth with 12.[^c15]

## The second time

For Barrichello the bitterest part was that he had been here before. A year earlier, at the same circuit, he had obeyed a team order on the final lap and handed Schumacher second place behind David Coulthard.[^c17] And shortly before the 2002 race he had signed a new Ferrari contract running to the end of 2004.[^c16]

The paddock did not mince its words. "It was absolutely disgraceful," said Renault team principal Flavio Briatore. Williams technical director Patrick Head called it "a cynical manoeuvre".[^c18]

## The story, years later

Barrichello only said more later. In 2008, on the Brazilian programme Fantástico, he claimed he was given an ultimatum on the final lap: "I was told to think about my contract."[^c19] In 2012, speaking to Globo, he called the finish "eight laps of war" and recalled screaming on the radio that he would not let his team-mate pass.[^c20]

Todt's account has shifted with time too. Twenty-four years ago he spoke of a decision passed on to the drivers eight laps from the end. In 2026, on the High Performance podcast, Todt said there had been an agreement before the start.[^c21] As he put it, the rule was: "if you are in front of Michael before the last pit stop, you must let Michael go". The embarrassment, Todt says, came from Rubens not wanting to stick to what had been agreed.[^c22]

## A million for the ceremony

On 26 June 2002 the FIA World Motor Sport Council heard the case. It could not punish the team orders themselves: both drivers were contractually bound to follow them, and the council described a team's right to decide its drivers' finishing order as "long-standing and traditional".[^c23] Breaching the podium procedure, Article 170 of the sporting regulations, was another matter, and it cost one million US dollars. The fine was imposed jointly on Ferrari, Schumacher and Barrichello: half payable immediately, the other half suspended for a year.[^c24]

FIA president Max Mosley conceded that "what happened in Austria was bad from a sporting point of view", but said the council had no legal grounds to punish it.[^c25] According to Mosley, the FIA received more faxes and emails from fans after the race than after Ayrton Senna's death in 1994.[^c26]

## A ban that lasted eight seasons

On 28 October 2002 the Formula One Commission wrote a short sentence into the rules: team orders which interfere with a race result are prohibited. It applied from the 2003 season.[^c27]

The 2002 championship never needed those four Austrian points. After the French Grand Prix on 21 July Schumacher had 96 points to Montoya's 34, with six rounds left. The title was his. He finished the year on 144 points to Barrichello's 77.[^c28]

Team orders did not disappear after the ban; they just got quieter. At the 2007 Brazilian Grand Prix Felipe Massa ceded a potential win to Kimi Räikkönen at the pit stops, and the Finn became champion.[^c29] In July 2010 at Hockenheim it happened in the open again. Massa's engineer Rob Smedley radioed: "Fernando is faster than you. Can you confirm you understood that message?" On lap 49 Fernando Alonso passed Massa.[^c30] The stewards fined Ferrari $100,000.[^c31]

This time the FIA decided not to fight what it could not police. On 10 December 2010, at a meeting in Monaco, the World Motor Sport Council deleted Article 39.1, which had banned team orders. Teams were simply reminded that actions bringing the sport into disrepute remain punishable under Article 151c of the International Sporting Code.[^c32]

So from 2011 what Ferrari did at Spielberg was once again within the rules. What is forbidden is doing it in a way that brings the sport into disrepute.[^c32]
