## There is no price tag

Ask a Formula 1 team what its car costs and you will not get a precise answer: teams keep part prices to themselves, so every figure below is an estimate by journalists and engineers.[^c1] Pat Symonds, a former Formula 1 chief technical director, who helped Motor Sport put together a cost breakdown of a car, began with an admission:

> "It's terribly difficult."[^c1]

Even the totals differ several times over. Red Bull estimated a car at roughly €15m in 2022, Motor Sport arrived at $20.6m in 2025, and Motorsport.com in 2023 quoted a "guesstimate" of around £6m, while warning that it was unclear what that figure included.[^c2] So what follows goes part by part, with a year and a source next to every number.

## The engine: the most expensive part

The power unit costs the most.[^c3] Customer teams do not buy engines one at a time: they pay the manufacturer for a season's supply, and the FIA caps that price. In the 2025 Technical Regulations the maximum supply price is €15m.[^c4] Motor Sport gave a different version in January 2025: the regulations supposedly call for €12m, while customers actually pay around €15m.[^c5]

[[media:m2]]

Where did €12m come from? In January 2016 Autosport reported that the four manufacturers had agreed to cut the supply price of the hybrid V6s to €12m a season. At the time, smaller teams were paying €20–25m a year.[^c6] The FIA's official decision in April 2016 was more cautious: €1m off in 2017 compared with 2016, and a further €3m off from 2018.[^c7]

New engines arrived in 2026, and the ceiling went up. The FIA regulations set a maximum of €22m adjusted for indexation, and €20.75m from 2027.[^c8] Back in the summer of 2025, Autosport, citing paddock sources, wrote that one major supplier had warned its customers that prices would double.[^c9]

How much one physical unit costs is a separate question, and this is where the estimates diverge most. Red Bull spoke of €10m per power unit in 2022. Motorsport.com in 2023 said around £3.5m to build a single unit and about £12m a year for several. Motor Sport in 2025 said €15m "for one engine".[^c10] Part of the gap is that some count the price of a season's contract covering several units, while others count the cost of a single power unit.[^c10]

## The monocoque

The monocoque is a single-piece carbon-composite shell that protects the driver. Motor Sport says it is usually made from 12 layers of carbon fibre mats.[^c11] Estimates: £500,000 from Motor Sport (2025), around €580,000 (£500,000) from PlanetF1 (2023), citing team estimates, and around £1m from Motorsport.com (2023).[^c12]

## The gearbox

The gearbox is semi-automatic, with eight forward gears and one reverse; some teams buy theirs from others, some make their own.[^c13] Estimates of its cost run from £250,000 (Motor Sport, 2025) and £275,000 (Symonds, 2021, as reported by PlanetF1) to $400,000 (Autosport, 2024) and even £750,000 (Motorsport.com, 2023).[^c14]

## Wings

The front wing is one of the most intricate parts of the car, and according to Symonds no part of the car has got more complicated since 2016.[^c33] He showed how the stakes have changed:

> "A Marussia front wing was £33,000."[^c15]

For a current wing he would go with "at least £100,000".[^c15] Haas team principal Guenther Steiner said in 2021 that "the front wing alone is a hundred and fifty thousand" dollars, and Motorsport.com in 2023 put it at £150,000.[^c16] Motor Sport puts the rear wing together with the DRS mechanism at £61,000–108,000.[^c17] The floor in the same breakdown costs around £100,000, and the hydraulics £123,000.[^c34]

## Steering wheel, halo, fuel tank and tyres

The steering wheel is carbon fibre, silicone grips and a lot of electronics.[^c18] Motor Sport put it at £35,000 in 2025, PlanetF1, citing Symonds' 2021 column, at €50,000 (£43,000), and Motorsport.com at £50,000 in 2023.[^c18] The halo, by Motor Sport's estimate, costs around £12,000 and the fuel tank around £22,000.[^c19]

[[media:m4]]

Symonds passed on what Pirelli's head of F1, Mario Isola, had told him: €600 per tyre.[^c20] Motor Sport puts a set at about $3,000.[^c20]

## Why "the price of a car" explains nothing

Motor Sport's breakdown covers manufacturing only: research and development are not included.[^c21] That is why Formula 1's rules limit a team's overall spending.

The cost cap was introduced in 2021: $145m, then $140m in 2022 and $135m from 2023.[^c22] From 2026 the cap is $215m if a season has no more than 24 rounds; each extra round adds $1.8m, plus indexation. The FIA itself converts this, for illustration, into £170m or €199m.[^c23] The jump is mostly an accounting one: many costs that used to be excluded were brought inside the cap, and F1 describes the change as roughly neutral.[^c24]

What does not count against the cap: marketing, driver pay together with their travel, the pay of the team's three highest-paid people, HR, finance and legal, property, bonuses up to a ceiling, FIA fees, and the power unit supply up to that same maximum price.[^c25] Car parts, on the other hand, "from the steering wheel to the wheel nuts", and spares are inside the cap.[^c26]

Engine manufacturers have their own separate cap: $95m a year in 2023–2025 and $190m from 2026.[^c27] Originally $130m was planned for 2026.[^c28]

## The cost of a crash

Teams pay for crash repairs out of their own cap, so a wrecked car is money that will be missing for upgrades.[^c36] Mercedes estimated the bill for Valtteri Bottas's car, wrecked at Imola in 2021, at around £1m.[^c29] Steiner, the same year, put the repairs after a Mick Schumacher crash at $300,000–500,000.[^c30] The Race calculated that three Williams crashes in early 2024 would cost the team more than $2m.[^c31]

## When a car does get sold

A Formula 1 car only gets a real price tag at auction. The Mercedes W04 in which Lewis Hamilton won the 2013 Hungarian Grand Prix was sold by RM Sotheby's in Las Vegas during the 2023 Grand Prix for $17.1m plus a 10% buyer's premium, about $18.8m (£15.1m) in total.[^c32] That is almost as much as Motor Sport's estimate for building a new car.[^c2][^c32] The buyer was paying for the history of that particular car. Older cars cost even more. In 2013, at the Goodwood Festival of Speed, Juan Manuel Fangio's 1954 Mercedes sold for £19.6m, a record at the time according to Autosport.[^c35] That record fell in February 2025: a 1954 Mercedes-Benz W 196 R Stromlinienwagen went for €51.155m at RM Sotheby's in Stuttgart, the most expensive Grand Prix car ever sold at auction.[^c37]
