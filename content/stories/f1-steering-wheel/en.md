## A control panel, not a hoop

An F1 car's steering wheel stopped being just a steering wheel long ago. Mercedes counted 25 buttons and switches on its 2019 wheel, plus the clutch and gearshift paddles.[^c1] Motor Sport counted differently in 2025: the wheel is built around standard McLaren Applied electronics, which allow up to 20 buttons, nine rotary switches and six paddles. What each button actually does is up to the team.[^c2]

## What is on it

Every team has its own layout, but the set of controls is similar.[^c10]

**Brakes.** On the Mercedes, five buttons and switches handled the brakes: the balance between front and rear axles, engine braking, and so-called brake migration, in which the balance changes depending on how hard the driver presses the pedal.[^c3] The driver tweaks the balance for specific corners.[^c32]

**Differential.** Another three switches on the Mercedes control the differential, meaning how torque is split between the rear wheels, separately for corner entry, apex and exit.[^c3] On the Ferrari SF90 wheel these are two rotaries, DIF IN and DIF MID, and drivers turn them several times a lap.[^c32]

**Engine.** Mercedes considered the "Strat" switch the most important: it sets the power unit modes, meaning engine performance and the deployment and recovery of electrical energy. Which mode to use is usually what the engineer tells the driver over the radio.[^c4] On the Ferrari the same job is done by "Manettino" switches, large rotaries like those on Ferrari road cars. Among them is "Grip", used at the start to set clutch slip, and a separate mode for the safety car.[^c5]

**Everyday buttons.** On the SF90, "N" selected neutral and "P" the pit lane speed limiter. There were buttons for the radio, DRS, an additional oil pump, the drinks bottle and confirming an instruction from the pit wall. Lights beside the screen repeat the marshals' flags.[^c5]

**Paddles.** Gears are changed with two paddles behind the wheel, usually left for down and right for up.[^c7] On a lap of Melbourne a Mercedes driver changed gear around 50 times, with a row of 15 LEDs above the screen showing the moment to shift.[^c6]

## What it is made of and what it costs

Mercedes named the main materials: carbon fibre, fibreglass, silicon, titanium and copper. They make up several hundred individual parts. The team built nearly everything itself in Brackley, apart from the central display and the circuit board beneath it: those are the same for every team.[^c8] The body is carbon, the grips silicone.[^c9]

The wheel is fitted to the individual driver's hands and is reworked even in mid-season.[^c10] Back in the 1980s Nigel Mansell, who put a lot of force through the wheel, asked for thicker grips, while Ayrton Senna, with his smoother style, wanted the opposite.[^c17] To avoid hitting the wrong thing in gloves and through the vibrations, Mercedes used aircraft-grade buttons with a stiff action and small plastic rims around some buttons. They helped most at the Monaco hairpin, where the wheel goes to full lock.[^c11]

Each Mercedes driver got three to four wheels a season, and building one took around 80 hours.[^c12] Teams do not publish prices, so there are only estimates: $50,000 (£35,000) from Motor Sport in 2025, €50,000 (£43,000) from PlanetF1 in 2023 and £50,000 from Motorsport.com.[^c13]

## How it got this way

In the 1960s the wheel of the McLaren M7C had one function: turning left and right.[^c14] The first switch arrived in the mid-1970s, an emergency ignition kill. The throttle slides of the time could jam open if dirt got into the mechanism, and the driver had to shut the engine off quickly.[^c14]

[[media:m3]]

In the 1980s the wheel got a quick-release mechanism: it could be taken off, and cockpits became tighter.[^c15] Radio and "boost" buttons appeared at the same time. Senna's 1988 McLaren wheel had only those two.[^c16] Stefan Johansson called the suede rim, which gave better feel of the car, the first "real improvement".[^c17]

[[media:m4]]

The turning point came in 1989. John Barnard's Ferrari 640 was the first car in Formula 1 with a semi-automatic gearbox and paddles behind the wheel. The gear lever and the clutch pedal were gone.[^c18] Barnard first proposed two buttons on the wheel; it was Piero Lardi Ferrari who suggested paddles.[^c19] On 26 March 1989 Nigel Mansell won the Brazilian Grand Prix in the 640 on its debut.[^c20] Gerhard Berger recalled:

> "For the first time, you had both hands on the steering wheel, you could shift in corners."[^c21]

According to Berger, a manual gearbox at Monaco meant around 1,500 gearshifts a race, with the driver steering one-handed 80% of the time.[^c21] By the mid-1990s every team had a semi-automatic gearbox. McLaren switched with the MP4/7 in 1992.[^c22]

Around then the wheel gained a pit lane speed limiter and rotary switches for settings.[^c23] The display long sat separately, on the front bulkhead of the cockpit, and on the McLaren it moved onto the wheel in 2009 to save weight.[^c23] A large LCD screen with menus of data arrived in 2014 along with the hybrid engines.[^c24]

## The start: the clutch at your fingertips

There is no clutch pedal; at the start the driver releases a paddle on the wheel. The 2026 regulations allow at most two clutch paddles, both pulled towards the driver, with no more than 80 mm of travel at the tip.[^c25] Since 2016 the FIA has banned paddles with a pre-programmed bite point, so the driver now finds the moment the clutch "bites" with their fingers.[^c25] At McLaren, Lando Norris practised starts while sitting in the car before going out: the lights on the wheel's screen go out as at a race start, and he releases the clutch exactly as far as the engineers ask.[^c27]

The rules also require a quick release: with belts fastened, the driver must remove the wheel and get out of the car within 7 seconds, then put the wheel back within 12 seconds in total.[^c26]

## One button, one ruined race

The 2021 Azerbaijan Grand Prix showed what a single mistake can cost. On the back of the Mercedes wheel was a "brake magic" button that instantly shifts nearly all braking to the front wheels. It is used to heat the front tyres on the formation lap or behind the safety car. At the lap-50 restart Lewis Hamilton hit it by accident and locked his wheels into Turn 1.[^c29] According to F1.com, the balance jumped from the usual 52–53% to the front axle to about 86.5%. Motorsport.com writes of 55–60% and about 90%.[^c28] Hamilton, who had been running second, finished fifteenth.[^c29]

## 2026: new buttons

From 2026 DRS is gone. Instead there is the "Boost Button", which the driver can press anywhere on the lap to get full power from the power unit, as long as there is charge. "Overtake Mode" is enabled if the driver is less than a second behind a rival at the detection point. On the following lap they then get an extra 0.5 MJ of energy. The driver opens the wings for "Straight Mode" by hand on each permitted straight.[^c31]
