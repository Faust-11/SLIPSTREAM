## Friday

The 1994 San Marino Grand Prix was the third round of the season. The race at Imola was scheduled for Sunday, 1 May.[^c1] The paddock was already tense: the FIA was struggling to police the ban on electronic driver aids, and there were suspicions about Benetton.[^c44]

On Friday, in the first qualifying session, Rubens Barrichello crashed his Jordan at the Variante Bassa chicane before the pits: the car was launched, hit the barrier and came to rest upside down.[^c2] Barrichello was knocked unconscious, but the medical teams were quickly with him. He took no further part in the weekend.[^c3] The fastest man that day was Ayrton Senna in the Williams.[^c4]

## Saturday: Roland Ratzenberger

About 18 minutes into Saturday's qualifying session, Roland Ratzenberger's Simtek hit the wall at the Villeneuve corner.[^c5] The most likely cause was a front wing failure.[^c6] According to Autosport, the wing came adrift because two of its four mounting bolts had worked loose.[^c45] Ratzenberger was killed.[^c7] By ESPN's count, he was the first person in 12 years to be killed at a grand prix.[^c8]

The Austrian was 33, and this was his first season in Formula 1. In Brazil he had failed to qualify; at the Pacific Grand Prix at Aida he finished 11th. Imola was only the third race weekend of his career.[^c9] His Simtek deal initially covered the first six races of the season.[^c46]

[[media:m2]]

The session was restarted 45 minutes after the accident, but Williams, Benetton and Sauber did not go out again.[^c10] Pole stayed with Senna, the 65th of his career. Michael Schumacher qualified second for Benetton.[^c11] Senna had driven himself to the scene of Ratzenberger's crash in a course car.[^c47] In a column published on race day, he wrote that his safety concerns had been "borne out in tragic fashion".[^c48]

## Sunday: Tamburello

Before the start the drivers held a minute's silence for Ratzenberger.[^c49] At the start Jyrki Järvilehto's Benetton stalled. Most drivers missed it, but Pedro Lamy's Lotus ran into the stationary car, and wheels and debris flew over the fence into the grandstand.[^c12] Sources differ on how many people were hurt there: GrandPrix.com says four, Motor Sport three spectators and a policeman with minor injuries, RaceFans nine spectators, one of them seriously.[^c13]

The race was not stopped: for four laps the field ran behind the safety car while the wreckage was cleared.[^c14] At the restart Senna led, with Schumacher right behind him.[^c15]

On lap seven (by GrandPrix.com's count, at the start of lap six) Senna's Williams went straight off at Tamburello and hit the concrete wall.[^c16] The race was stopped.[^c17] Senna was flown by helicopter to the Maggiore hospital in Bologna. He died that evening.[^c18] At 6.40pm the organisers confirmed his death.[^c19]

The race itself had been restarted that afternoon, after a delay of about 45 minutes.[^c20] According to GrandPrix.com, few in the paddock understood how serious Senna's injuries were, and no one objected to the restart.[^c21] There was another accident in the pit lane: a wheel came off Michele Alboreto's Minardi and injured Ferrari mechanics and a Lotus mechanic.[^c22] Schumacher won, Nicola Larini was second for Ferrari and Mika Häkkinen third for McLaren. Damon Hill finished sixth in the other Williams.[^c23]

## Monaco: two weeks later

The next round, at Monaco, began with another crash. On Thursday Karl Wendlinger crashed his Sauber at the chicane, suffered head injuries and fell into a coma.[^c24] The same day FIA president Max Mosley announced sweeping rule changes.[^c25]

Williams entered a single car at Monaco. The front row of the grid was left empty, and a minute's silence was held before the race.[^c26] Schumacher won.[^c58]

The drivers, meanwhile, revived the Grand Prix Drivers' Association, the GPDA.[^c27] At a meeting in Monaco chaired by Martin Brundle, Michael Schumacher, Gerhard Berger and Christian Fittipaldi were named as its representatives, with Niki Lauda to act as official spokesman for the rest of the year.[^c28]

## What changed

The first job was to slow the cars. "The immediate work was not to improve the specific safety of the cars, but rather to slow the cars down," Pat Symonds told the F1 website.[^c29]

The changes came race by race. From Monaco there was a pit-lane speed limit,[^c30] 80km/h according to RaceFans.[^c31] For the Spanish Grand Prix the diffuser was shortened and the front wing endplates changed to cut downforce, and a temporary chicane was added at Barcelona.[^c32] For Canada, holes were cut in the airboxes to take away some engine power.[^c33] For the German Grand Prix a wooden plank was fitted under the car, which meant the cars had to be raised.[^c34] In 1995 engine capacity was cut from 3.5 to 3.0 litres.[^c35] Driver protection was improved too: in 1995 crash-test speeds and loads were increased and a side-impact test was added.[^c56] The cockpit sides were raised, but sources give different years: Motorsport.com says 1995 (from 400 to 550mm), Autosport 1996.[^c57]

Imola itself changed. Before the 1995 season a chicane was built at Tamburello on the inside of the corner: the outside wall could not be moved because there is a river behind it. Another chicane appeared before the Villeneuve corner, where Ratzenberger died.[^c36]

## The courts

In Italy, prosecutors investigated Senna's death. In December 1995 a panel of experts chaired by Professor Enrico Lorenzini of Bologna University produced a 500-page technical report: it named a steering failure caused by a faulty weld as the most likely cause of the accident.[^c50] The prosecution argued that a poorly modified steering column had broken and Senna had lost control.[^c37] The defendants included team owner Frank Williams, technical director Patrick Head and designer Adrian Newey.[^c38] For Head and Newey the prosecutor asked for one-year suspended sentences.[^c51]

In December 1997 the court in Imola acquitted them.[^c39] It did not, however, rule on the cause of the crash, so the prosecutors appealed.[^c52] In November 1999 the appeal court upheld the acquittals.[^c40] Judge Francesco Agnoli said the prosecution's claims offered "no proof of blame".[^c53] Benetton had even said it would boycott races in Italy if there were convictions.[^c54] That was not the end of it: Italy's Court of Cassation found procedural errors and sent the case back for a new hearing.[^c41] When the new trial opened in Bologna, the prosecutor immediately asked for the case to be closed.[^c55] It finally ended in May 2005. Newey was acquitted, and the case against Head was dropped under the statute of limitations.[^c42]

## Two farewells

Gerhard Berger flew to Ratzenberger's funeral in Salzburg straight from Senna's memorial in São Paulo.[^c43]
