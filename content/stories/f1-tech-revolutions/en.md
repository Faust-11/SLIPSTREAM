Formula 1 has always been a contest between engineers as much as between drivers. We have picked twelve ideas after which a racing car could never be the same again. Some became the standard for the whole grid, others were banned within months, and some of their authors simply managed to win while rivals were still arguing about the rulebook. The order is chronological: that way you can see how one revolution leads to the next.

## 1. Cooper: the engine behind the driver

Cooper was run by John Cooper and his father Charles, and they were the ones who moved the engine behind the driver.[^c1] The logic was simple: with no propeller shaft running beneath the driver, he could sit lower and the car's frontal area shrank. Doug Nye of Motor Sport explains what that gain was worth:

[[media:m2]]

> "One square foot less frontal area was considered worth 25bhp."[^c2]

The first world championship wins for cars with the engine behind the driver went to Cooper: Stirling Moss won the 1958 Argentine Grand Prix and Maurice Trintignant won Monaco that same season.[^c3] The principle itself was not new: Auto Union had won with it in the 1930s.[^c4] In 1959 the Cooper T51 gave Jack Brabham the title and the team the constructors' crown, with five wins from nine rounds.[^c5] The season finale was pure cinema: at Sebring Brabham ran out of fuel and pushed the car himself for the last 400 yards.[^c6]

**What happened next:** after 1960, no front-engined car won a championship race or a title again.[^c7]

## 2. Lotus 25: a monocoque instead of a frame

Until 1962 a racing car was built on a tubular spaceframe. Colin Chapman did away with the frame: the Lotus 25's body was an aluminium structure that carried the loads itself.[^c8] Chapman, incidentally, disliked the word "monocoque" and called his chassis a "bathtub", and similar load-bearing structures had already been seen in aviation and on the Jaguar D-type sports car.[^c9] But it was the Lotus 25 that made the principle the norm in Formula 1.[^c10]

The gain came in both weight and stiffness: the tub weighed around 32kg (70lb), against more than 100lb for the frame of its predecessor, the Lotus 24, and it was considerably stiffer.[^c11] Its debut at the 1962 Dutch Grand Prix ended with Jim Clark only ninth.[^c12] The following season, though, he won seven of the ten races in the Lotus 25, scored the maximum possible 54 points and became champion, while Lotus took its first constructors' title.[^c13]

**What happened next:** of the major teams, only BRM responded straight away, while Cooper, Brabham and Ferrari stuck with tubes for several more seasons.[^c14] Eventually the aluminium monocoque became everyone's standard for two decades.[^c15]

## 3. Wings: air pushes the car down

Formula 1 borrowed the idea of an inverted wing pressing the car into the track from Jim Hall: his Chaparral sports cars had raced with a tall strut-mounted wing since 1966.[^c16] Who brought it into grand prix racing first, however, is where sources disagree. F1.com and Autosport date it from Monaco 1968, where Chapman fitted the first aerodynamic surfaces to the Lotus 49B and Graham Hill won from pole.[^c17] Nigel Roebuck of Motor Sport holds that a grand prix car first raced with a wing at the next round, at Spa, on Chris Amon's Ferrari; Goodwood adds that a wing appeared on the Brabham there too.[^c18]

[[media:m3]]

After Spa, high strut-mounted wings became a must-have, and lap times dropped by several seconds.[^c19] The price was steep: at the 1969 Spanish Grand Prix at Montjuïc the wings on both Lotus 49s collapsed, and Hill and Jochen Rindt had heavy crashes.[^c20]

**What happened next:** at the very next round, in Monaco, the CSI, the governing body of the day, banned unrestricted wings in the middle of the weekend.[^c21] Wings stayed, but designers moved to solutions integrated into the bodywork.[^c22]

## 4. Tyrrell P34: six wheels

Tyrrell's chief designer Derek Gardner fitted four small front wheels in place of two large ones.[^c23] The aim was less drag and less front-end lift, so the front wing could be made more modest.[^c24] Gardner wanted nine-inch wheels; he and Goodyear settled on ten inches, "Mini-size".[^c25]

The P34 made its debut at the 1976 Spanish Grand Prix at Jarama, the fourth round of the season.[^c26] Then at Anderstorp Jody Scheckter won the Swedish Grand Prix from pole, with Patrick Depailler finishing second.[^c27] It remains the only win for a six-wheeled car in Formula 1.[^c28] Scheckter remembers it fondly:

> "It was a fun car to drive."[^c29]

**What happened next:** in 1977 the project faded because of tyres. Gardner explained it like this:

> "By 1977 Goodyear were supplying the whole of F1 but the tiny front tyres were for one team only."[^c30]

At the end of 1977 Tyrrell abandoned the concept, and six-wheelers were later banned altogether.[^c31]

## 5. Renault RS01: turbo

The rules of the time allowed turbocharged engines of half the capacity, 1.5 litres, alongside three-litre naturally aspirated engines.[^c32] Renault was the first to take this seriously: the RS01 made its debut at the 1977 British Grand Prix at Silverstone.[^c33] Its first race lasted 16 laps before Jean-Pierre Jabouille retired with a turbo failure.[^c34] Because of the clouds of steam and smoke whenever it broke, sceptics nicknamed the car the "yellow teapot".[^c35]

[[media:m4]]

The win came on 1 July 1979: Jabouille won the French Grand Prix at Dijon, and it was the first victory for a turbo car.[^c36] He won in the RS01's successor, the RS10.[^c37]

**What happened next:** everyone took up the turbo. In 1982 Ferrari won the first constructors' title with a turbo engine, and in 1983 Nelson Piquet in a Brabham BMW became the first world champion in a turbo car.[^c38] Renault itself never won a title in the turbo era.[^c39] At the end of 1988 turbos were banned as too powerful, too dangerous and too expensive.[^c40] They returned in 2014 as 1.6-litre hybrid V6s.[^c41]

## 6. Lotus 78 and 79: ground effect

Chapman's next idea was to turn the entire underside of the car into a wing. Lotus 78 designer Ralph Bellamy and Peter Wright found in the Imperial College wind tunnel that an inverted aerofoil close to the moving belt was literally sucked down onto it.[^c42] The Lotus 78's sidepods had a venturi profile, and skirts along the edges sealed the underbody so that the low pressure acted across its full width.[^c43] In 1977 the Lotus 78 won five races.[^c44]

In 1978 came the Lotus 79. It made its world championship debut at Zolder, where Mario Andretti took pole and won.[^c45] Over the season Lotus won eight races, Andretti became champion and the team took the constructors' title.[^c46]

[[media:m5]]

**What happened next:** in 1981, to rein in cornering speeds, sliding skirts were banned, and from 1983 flat bottoms became mandatory.[^c47] Ground effect returned with the 2022 rules.[^c48]

## 7. Brabham BT46B: the "vacuum cleaner"

Gordon Murray's Brabham BT46B had a large fan at the back that sucked air out from under the floor; skirts held the low pressure, and the car was pulled down onto the asphalt.[^c49] The official story sounded innocent: the fan was there to cool the engine.[^c50] Movable aerodynamic devices were banned, but the CSI measured that more than 55% of the fan's power went to cooling and declared the car legal.[^c51]

[[media:m6]]

On 17 June 1978 at Anderstorp, Niki Lauda won the Swedish Grand Prix by 34 seconds.[^c52] That race remained the BT46B's only one.[^c53] The final version of the fan went to Sweden untested, Murray recalls:

> "I had just enough time to machine two hubs from solid aluminium."[^c54]

**What happened next:** the car was not banned: Brabham owner Bernie Ecclestone withdrew it himself.[^c55] The Race puts this down to politics, and Motor Sport also allows for that version: Ecclestone had just become head of the constructors' association FOCA and needed his rivals' support.[^c56] Rule changes that closed off the concept were in force from 1979.[^c57]

## 8. McLaren MP4/1: the carbon-fibre monocoque

John Barnard needed a very narrow chassis. A steel monocoque proved too heavy, while carbon fibre was strong and light.[^c58] Barnard got his cue on a visit to British Aerospace at Weybridge.[^c59] The MP4/1's monocoque was made by the American company Hercules in Utah, and the first chassis was assembled from five major components.[^c60]

[[media:m7]]

Whether McLaren was first depends on whom you ask. McLaren, Autosport and F1.com call the MP4/1 the first car with a monocoque made entirely of carbon fibre.[^c61] Lotus insists that its Type 88 with a carbon-fibre chassis came earlier, but protests from rivals meant it was never allowed to race.[^c62]

On 18 July 1981 John Watson won the British Grand Prix in the MP4/1, McLaren's first victory since 1977.[^c63] The decisive argument came at Monza: in September Watson crashed the car at around 140mph and walked away unhurt.[^c64] Barnard summed it up:

> "That disproved all the knockers."[^c65]

**What happened next:** carbon-fibre monocoques became the norm for the entire grid.[^c66]

## 9. Ferrari 640: the semi-automatic gearbox

The 1989 Ferrari 640 was the first in Formula 1 to get a semi-automatic sequential gearbox, designed by John Barnard.[^c67] According to Motorsport.com, Barnard did it mainly for packaging and aerodynamic reasons: the cockpit could be made narrower.[^c68] F1.com and Autosport stress something else: the gearbox was put under less strain and the engine was not over-revved.[^c69]

[[media:m8]]

The system was raw. Gerhard Berger recalls:

> "The downside was it never worked more than five laps."[^c70]

Even so, on its debut at the Brazilian Grand Prix on 26 March 1989, Nigel Mansell started sixth and won.[^c71]

**What happened next:** for the better part of three decades every F1 car has run with steering-wheel paddles and a sequential gearbox.[^c72]

## 10. Williams FW14B: active suspension

Active suspension works through hydraulics with electronic control and adjusts the ride height at each corner of the car.[^c73] The father of the Lotus system was aerodynamicist Peter Wright; the Lotus 92 took to the track with it in 1983.[^c74] The first win for an active car came from Ayrton Senna in a Lotus at Monaco in 1987.[^c75]

Williams brought the idea to perfection. Patrick Head explained that the main point was not a softer ride but control of the aerodynamic platform.[^c76] From 1990 development was led by Paddy Lowe.[^c77] In 1992 the FW14B took 15 poles in 16 races and 10 wins, Nigel Mansell became champion and Williams took the constructors' title.[^c78]

[[media:m9]]

**What happened next:** from 1994 active suspension was banned along with other electronic driver aids.[^c79] According to Head, at Hockenheim in 1993 Max Mosley and Bernie Ecclestone gave team principals an ultimatum: agree to ban the new technologies.[^c80]

## 11. Brawn BGP 001: the double diffuser

Brawn, Toyota and Williams read the 2009 rules literally: holes between the planes of the floor allowed air to be fed into a second, upper deck of the diffuser.[^c81] Accounts differ on who came up with the loophole. F1.com names junior aerodynamicist Masayuki Minagawa, Autosport a Japanese aerodynamics group within Honda, and Motorsport.com former Super Aguri designers.[^c82]

Brawn GP grew out of the Honda team.[^c83] Its BGP 001 won on debut: Jenson Button took pole and victory at the 2009 Australian Grand Prix, with Rubens Barrichello second.[^c84] Rivals protested, but on 15 April 2009 the FIA International Court of Appeal ruled the Brawn, Toyota and Williams diffusers legal.[^c85] Brawn won eight of the seventeen races, Button became champion and the team took the constructors' title.[^c86]

**What happened next:** rivals fitted their own versions over the course of 2009, and from 2011 the loophole was closed.[^c87]

## 12. Halo: the titanium hoop

The halo is a titanium hoop over the driver's head weighing around 6–7kg.[^c88] Autosport links its arrival to the death of Jules Bianchi.[^c89] It became mandatory in 2018 and initially met strong opposition.[^c90] The strength requirements are severe: among others, 116kN vertically downwards; at the Cranfield laboratory the hoop was tested to more than 100kN.[^c91] Mercedes' James Allison compared this to the weight of a London double-decker bus.[^c92]

The halo passed its first serious test as early as 2018 at Spa: Fernando Alonso's car flew over Charles Leclerc, who was unhurt.[^c93] In Bahrain in 2020 Romain Grosjean's car was split in half in the barrier, and the halo protected the driver's head.[^c94] Ross Brawn said:

[[media:m10]]

> "There's absolutely no doubt the halo was the factor that saved the day."[^c95]

Grosjean, who had once been against it, called the halo "the greatest thing we've brought to Formula 1".[^c96] And after his crash at Monza in 2021 Lewis Hamilton said: "Thank god for the halo. That ultimately saved me."[^c97]

**What happened next:** in 2020 IndyCar got its own form of closed-cockpit protection, the aeroscreen.[^c98]

One pattern keeps repeating in this list: what gets banned often comes back. Turbos have been back in F1 since 2014,[^c41] ground effect since 2022.[^c48]
