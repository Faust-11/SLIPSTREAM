## Flimsy cars, unforgiving circuits

Motor Sport sums up the era in which Jackie Stewart raced in a single phrase: flimsy cars on ballsy circuits. Over his career Stewart lost dozens of friends to the sport; by Motor Sport's count, more than fifty.[^c1] Among them was Jochen Rindt, who was killed at Monza in 1970.[^c2] The title that season still went to Rindt.[^c3]

This is the story of how Formula 1 became safer step by step: circuits, medicine, car design and drivers' equipment.

## Spa, 1966: the crash that changed everything

On 12 June 1966, on the opening lap of the Belgian Grand Prix at Spa, a sudden downpour hit the circuit and Stewart's BRM went off the track.[^c4] He was trapped in the wrecked car while fuel burned his skin.[^c5] "There were no officials at all," Stewart recalled.[^c6]

He was rescued by Graham Hill, his BRM team-mate, and Bob Bondurant, who had both gone off on the same flooded stretch.[^c7] According to Jalopnik they had no tools, so they borrowed a wrench from a spectator, removed the steering wheel and pulled Stewart out.[^c8] From then on, the same outlet writes, Stewart taped a wrench to his steering wheel.[^c9]

> "It was at that time that I woke up to try and do something with the Grand Prix Drivers' Association."[^c10]

Stewart pushed for seat belts and full-face helmets, even though he had first been against belts himself. "I thought seat belts were unnecessary and uncomfortable, at first," he admitted.[^c11] The campaign made him enemies: part of the paddock and the press felt the drivers had taken too much power.[^c12]

## Boycotts

The drivers' strongest weapon was refusing to race. In 1969 the Belgian Grand Prix at Spa did not take place: the circuit lacked the crash barriers the drivers had asked for.[^c13]

The following year it was the Nürburgring's turn. The organisers had not completed the promised safety work and the Grand Prix Drivers' Association refused to race there, so the 1970 German Grand Prix moved to Hockenheim for the first time.[^c14] Stewart made an impassioned plea to his fellow drivers not to race if the demands were not met.[^c15] Rindt won at Hockenheim.[^c16]

## The Prof

In 1978 Bernie Ecclestone offered neurosurgeon Sid Watkins the role of Formula 1 medical delegate.[^c17] Classic & Sports Car recalls that circuit medical centres at the time were often converted buses, with no anaesthetists on site.[^c18]

In September 1978 Ronnie Peterson was killed after a crash at the start at Monza.[^c19] Afterwards Watkins insisted that on the first lap of every race he would ride in a fully equipped medical car, so he could reach any accident as quickly as possible.[^c20] He secured common standards for medical centres and a mandatory medical helicopter at every race.[^c21]

Watkins spent 26 years in Formula 1.[^c22] Mika Häkkinen, Gerhard Berger and Rubens Barrichello are among the drivers he is credited with helping to save.[^c23] His hardest weekend was Imola 1994, where Roland Ratzenberger and Ayrton Senna, a close friend of the Prof, were killed.[^c24] After Imola Watkins chaired the FIA Expert Advisory Safety Committee and later became the first president of the FIA Institute for Motor Sport Safety.[^c25] He died in September 2012, aged 84.[^c26]

## Carbon fibre and crash tests

In 1981 John Barnard's McLaren MP4/1 became the first Formula 1 car with a monocoque made entirely of carbon composite.[^c27] Monza won over the sceptics: at the 1981 Italian Grand Prix John Watson crashed his MP4/1 at around 140mph (roughly 225km/h) and walked away.[^c28]

[[media:m3]]

Before 1985 no crash tests were required at all; that year a frontal impact requirement arrived, and tests were added and tightened year after year.[^c29]

The real turning point came after Imola 1994. The FIA went straight to work on circuits: computer analysis identified 27 high-risk corners at F1-grade tracks, pit lane speed limits were reduced and helmet standards were tightened.[^c30] In 1996 came higher cockpit sides and side headrests, in 1998 a requirement that a driver must be able to get out of the car within 10 seconds, and in 1999 tethers to keep the wheels attached.[^c31]

## HANS and the Halo

The HANS device, which supports the head and neck in an impact, was designed by engineer Robert Hubbard; the idea came from his relative, sportscar racer Jim Downing.[^c32] In Formula 1 it became mandatory from 1 January 2003.[^c33] According to the FIA, HANS has virtually eliminated catastrophic injuries to the craniovertebral junction, previously a common cause of death in racing.[^c34] The FIA writes that HANS played a key role when Robert Kubica had his huge crash at the 2007 Canadian Grand Prix.[^c49]

The head remained the most exposed part. In qualifying for the 2009 Hungarian Grand Prix Felipe Massa was hit by a spring and suffered a fractured skull.[^c50] From 2011 the FIA required helmets to be reinforced above the visor with a Zylon panel.[^c51]

On 5 October 2014, at the Japanese Grand Prix at Suzuka, Jules Bianchi suffered a severe crash; he died on 17 July 2015.[^c35] After his accident the FIA introduced the Virtual Safety Car, which since 2015 has forced all drivers to slow down.[^c36] In 2017 The Guardian noted that Bianchi was the only driver killed on track in Formula 1 since 1994.[^c37]

Head protection was the next step. The Halo, a hoop over the cockpit, was unpopular: at the F1 Strategy Group meeting in July 2017, according to The Guardian, only one team supported it. The FIA answered with numbers: by its figures, the Halo increases the chance of surviving a crash by 17%.[^c38] The device became mandatory from the 2018 season.[^c39]

The test came quickly. On the first lap of the 2018 Belgian Grand Prix Fernando Alonso's McLaren was launched over Charles Leclerc's Sauber, and the Halo appeared to protect Leclerc.[^c40] After the season FIA president Jean Todt said the Halo "demonstrated it was a good decision".[^c41]

## Bahrain, 2020: 27 seconds

On 29 November 2020, on the first lap of the Bahrain Grand Prix, Romain Grosjean's Haas hit the barrier at 192km/h with a peak load of 67g.[^c42] The car split in two and caught fire, and the survival cell pierced the barrier.[^c43]

The FIA Medical Car reached the scene within 11 seconds.[^c44] Grosjean got out unaided and was clear of the car 27 seconds after impact, with burns to his hands.[^c45] The FIA concluded that the helmet, HANS, harness, survival cell, seat, headrest and Halo all performed as they should.[^c46]

The investigation into that crash triggered another large-scale safety review, and after Zhou Guanyu's car flipped at Silverstone in 2022, where the Halo again protected the driver, the FIA strengthened the requirements for roll hoops.[^c47]

Stewart, who once lay in a wrecked BRM with no marshal in sight, puts modern Formula 1 like this:

> "But now, we have a cockpit of a car that's a survival cell. We've never been better prepared."[^c48]
