## December 2008: Honda shuts the door

On 5 December 2008 Honda announced that the 2008 season had been its last in Formula 1.[^c1] Company president Takeo Fukui explained that conditions for the global car industry were deteriorating fast and the core business had to be protected.[^c2] The team, with its base in Brackley and around 700 staff,[^c3] was put up for sale.[^c4]

In sporting terms there was little to mourn: in 2008 Honda scored 14 points and finished ninth in the constructors' standings.[^c5] The next day Fukui made it clear the Japanese would not haggle:

> "Just to make the team possible to exist, a small price tag is acceptable."[^c6]

According to Autosport, the money to keep going was guaranteed only until March.[^c7] That gave the people in Brackley roughly three months to find a buyer or go home. The stakes were high: Bernie Ecclestone put the cost of running a Formula 1 team at around £200 million a year.[^c42] The talks were led by team principal Ross Brawn and chief executive Nick Fry.[^c8] In public Fry stayed upbeat and pitched the team as a prize:

> "This team is one of the best funded, has the best assets, the best resources in the pit lane."[^c39]

Autosport reported that three prospective buyers had shown interest.[^c38]

## March 2009: a team for a token sum

In the end the team did not go to an outside investor. On 6 March 2009 Honda announced it had sold all the shares in Honda Racing F1 Team to Ross Brawn himself.[^c9] Tokyo did not name a price. Motor Sport wrote at the time of a "nominal" fee, and later the press put a figure on it that became part of the legend: one pound sterling.[^c10] In effect the insiders were buying: five Honda Racing directors, Fry among them, took part in the buyout.[^c11]

[[media:m2]]

Honda did not leave the team penniless: according to Motor Sport, it left behind a "substantial dowry" which, together with payments from FOM, was to keep the operation afloat in the first months.[^c12] But Honda was pulling out of all its Formula 1 activities, so a new engine had to be found.[^c1] The new supplier was in England: Mercedes-Benz High Performance Engines of Brixworth, the same engines that powered McLaren and Force India.[^c13] Mercedes was chosen over Ferrari because it was easier to fit into a chassis designed around the Honda.[^c14]

The drivers stayed the same as in 2008: Jenson Button and Rubens Barrichello.[^c15] At the launch Brawn chose his words carefully:

> "The journey ahead will be challenging but exciting."[^c16]

## The double diffuser

In 2009 Formula 1 got a new technical rulebook, and it limited the size of the diffuser under the rear of the car. But the way the rules checked those dimensions left room for channels between the floor's planes. Through them, air fed a second, upper deck of the diffuser, and the car gained extra downforce without formally breaking any rule.[^c17] At the start of the season three cars ran the layout: the Brawn BGP 001, the Toyota TF109 and the Williams FW31.[^c18]

Brawn engineer John Owen later said the idea itself came from Japanese aerodynamicist Masayuki Minagawa, known in the team simply as "Mina".[^c19] The same Owen punctures the biggest myth about that car. By his account, had the diffuser been removed before the first race, the car would have lost only 0.3 seconds a lap.[^c20] In other words, the BGP 001 was quick without the trick.

Its rivals saw it differently. Protests were lodged in Melbourne, but on 26 March the Australian Grand Prix stewards declared all three cars legal.[^c21] An appeal followed. It was brought by Ferrari, Red Bull and Renault, with McLaren and BMW Sauber backing them. That is how the full text of the decision and Autosport describe it; Sky Sports, by contrast, listed BMW Sauber among the appellants themselves.[^c22] The case was heard in Paris, and on 15 April 2009 the FIA International Court of Appeal threw out all the appeals.[^c23] The wording was brief:

> "the Stewards were correct to find that the cars in question comply with the applicable regulations."[^c24]

The other teams had only one option left: build their own double diffusers, fast. The loophole was finally closed only from the 2011 season.[^c25]

## Six out of seven

On 29 March 2009 in Melbourne, Button took pole, Barrichello qualified second, and they finished the race in the same order.[^c26] It was not easy: both Brawn drivers made mistakes, and a few laps from the end a one-two looked unlikely. It was settled by the collision between Sebastian Vettel and Robert Kubica on lap 55.[^c40] Winning first time out is extremely rare in Formula 1. RacingNews365 counted only five teams that have done it, and the last debutant to score a one-two before Brawn was Mercedes at the 1954 French Grand Prix.[^c27]

[[media:m3]]

Button went on to win in Malaysia, Bahrain, Spain, Monaco and Turkey. The only exception was China, where he finished third. That made six wins in the first seven races.[^c28] Nick Fry later admitted how modest the early expectations had been:

> "At the beginning of the year if someone had said sign up for two or three wins, we would have taken the money."[^c30]

The second half of the season was a different story. After Turkey Button did not win again, and the team's two further victories came from Barrichello, in Valencia and Monza.[^c29]

## Interlagos

At the Brazilian Grand Prix on 18 October 2009, Button started only 14th but fought his way up to fifth. That was enough to make him world champion, and in the same race Brawn also clinched the constructors' title.[^c31] Barrichello, who had started from pole, came home only eighth that day.[^c41] After the flag Brawn did not hold back:

[[media:m4]]

> "he drove a sensational race"[^c33]

Button himself remembered where it all started:

> "When I first jumped in a car 21 years ago I never expected to be world champion"[^c32]

In the final standings Button had 95 points, 11 more than Sebastian Vettel. Brawn ended the season on 172 points to Red Bull's 153.5 and won eight of the seventeen races.[^c34]

## The sale

The fairy tale lasted exactly one season. On 16 November 2009 Daimler and the investment fund Aabar Investments announced they were buying 75.1% of Brawn GP: 45.1% to Daimler, 30% to Aabar, with 24.9% staying with the existing owners.[^c35] The team was to become Mercedes Grand Prix.[^c36] It would still be run by Ross Brawn.[^c37]

Less than a year passed between Honda's announcement and the sale to Mercedes.[^c1][^c35] In that time the team that had been handed over for a token sum changed its name, its engine and its owner, and won both championships along the way.
