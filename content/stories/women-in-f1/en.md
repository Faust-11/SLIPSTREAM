## Five entries, two starts

In the whole history of the World Championship, five women have entered a Grand Prix, and only two of them made it to the start of a race.[^c1] Several more drivers later took the wheel of Formula 1 cars in practice sessions and tests. This is their story: who got through the door, where they were stopped, and why the conversation about women in F1 is still not over.

## Maria Teresa de Filippis: the first

Naples-born Maria Teresa de Filippis took up racing because of a bet: her brothers wagered that she could not drive fast.[^c2] The brothers lost.

Her first attempt in Formula 1 was the 1958 Monaco Grand Prix, where she failed to qualify.[^c3] Her debut came on 15 June 1958 at the Belgian Grand Prix at Spa: de Filippis started 19th in a private Maserati 250F and finished tenth, two laps behind the winner.[^c4] That season she started three World Championship races.[^c5]

[[media:m2]]

The attitude she faced in the paddock is captured by a story she told The Observer in 2006. When she was prevented from racing at the French Grand Prix, the race director allegedly said: "The only helmet a woman should wear is the one at the hairdresser's."[^c6]

In 1959, after the death of her team boss Jean Behra, de Filippis walked away from the sport.[^c7] She died in January 2016, aged 89.[^c8]

## Lella Lombardi: half a point nobody has matched

Lella Lombardi, a butcher's daughter, learned to drive at the wheel of the family's delivery van.[^c9] In 1974 she tried to qualify a year-old Brabham BT42 for the British Grand Prix at Brands Hatch, but missed the cut.[^c10] Sponsorship backing then opened the door to the works March team for the 1975 season.[^c11]

At the very first round, in South Africa, Lombardi became the first woman since de Filippis to qualify for a Grand Prix.[^c12]

The biggest day of her career came at one of the darkest races of the decade. On 27 April 1975, on the Montjuïc street circuit in Barcelona, race leader Rolf Stommelen lost his rear wing and his car flew over the barrier. Sources differ on the death toll: Motor Sport and PlanetF1 give four people, Autosport and RacingNews365 five.[^c13]

The race was stopped after 29 laps. Lombardi was running sixth at that moment, two laps down, and because the distance had not been completed only half points were awarded: 0.5 for her.[^c14] Victory went to Jochen Mass, the only Grand Prix win of his career.[^c15]

That half point still makes Lombardi the only woman ever to score in the World Championship.[^c16] She herself made little of it. "I don't think it dawned on me that I was the very first woman to collect championship points," she told The New York Times.[^c17] She put it differently when talking about herself:

> "I hope that people now understand that I am a racer who is a woman, rather than a woman who races."[^c18]

That season she also finished seventh at the German Grand Prix.[^c19] The rest of the year went badly: Lombardi complained about her car's strange handling, but nobody listened. When the chassis passed to Ronnie Peterson in 1976 he reported the same thing, and a crack was found in a bulkhead of the monocoque.[^c20] By then March had already replaced Lombardi with Peterson.[^c21]

[[media:m3]]

Lombardi's last race was the 1976 Austrian Grand Prix, where she finished twelfth. No woman has started a World Championship race since.[^c22] In total she made 12 starts.[^c23] Lombardi died of breast cancer in 1992, aged 50.[^c24]

## Divina Galica, Desiré Wilson and Giovanna Amati

Britain's Divina Galica came to motor racing from alpine skiing: she captained the British women's Olympic team in 1968 and 1972.[^c25] Her first ever race came in 1974, when Brands Hatch promoter John Webb invited her to a celebrity race, and Galica finished second.[^c26] She entered the 1976 British Grand Prix in a Surtees TS16 but did not qualify.[^c27] That weekend remains the only one in which a Formula 1 entry list included two women: Lombardi was also trying to make the grid.[^c28] In 1978 Galica tried twice more with Hesketh, in Argentina and Brazil, and failed to qualify both times.[^c29]

South Africa's Desiré Wilson[^c30] holds a record of a different kind. In April 1980 at Brands Hatch she won the Evening News Trophy, a round of the British Aurora AFX championship for Formula 1 cars, leading from start to finish in her Wolf.[^c31] It was not a World Championship event, but it remains the only win by a woman in a race for Formula 1 cars.[^c32] Did she feel she was making history? "It never even crossed my mind," Wilson told Motor Sport.[^c33]

Three months later, at the same circuit, she entered the British Grand Prix in a Williams FW07, her only World Championship entry, and failed to make the grid.[^c34] In 1981 she did start the South African Grand Prix in a Tyrrell, but because of the FISA–FOCA war that race did not count towards the championship.[^c35]

Italy's Giovanna Amati got a Brabham seat for 1992 after Japan's Akihiko Nakaya failed to obtain a superlicence.[^c36] She entered the first three rounds of the season, in South Africa, Mexico and Brazil, and failed to qualify every time.[^c37] Brabham then replaced her with Damon Hill.[^c38] The 1992 Brazilian Grand Prix at Interlagos remains the last time a woman tried to qualify for a Grand Prix.[^c39]

## Practice, tests and F1 Academy

The next woman to appear at an official Formula 1 weekend came 22 years later. On 4 July 2014 Susie Wolff drove in first practice for the British Grand Prix in Valtteri Bottas's Williams. Her session did not last long: after four laps the engine lost oil pressure.[^c40] Wolff later drove in practice at Hockenheim that year, and in 2015 at Barcelona and Silverstone.[^c41] In 2014 Simona de Silvestro, as Sauber's affiliated driver, tested the team's machinery.[^c42]

On 30 October 2018 Colombia's Tatiana Calderón, Sauber's test driver, completed 23 laps, or 100 km, in the C37 in Mexico City during a promotional filming day.[^c43]

The biggest change of recent years is F1 Academy, a Formula 4-level women's series for drivers aged 16 to 25 that Formula 1 launched in 2023.[^c44] Its first champion, in October 2023, was Spain's Marta García with Prema.[^c45] Since 2024 each of the ten Formula 1 teams has backed a driver in the series.[^c46] F1 Academy is run by Susie Wolff.[^c47]

The 2025 F1 Academy champion Doriane Pin covered 76 laps at Silverstone in a Mercedes W12 in April 2026, becoming the first woman to drive a Formula 1 car for that team.[^c48] In September 2026 Jamie Chadwick, a three-time W Series champion, drove 32 laps at Portimão in last year's Williams.[^c49]

[[media:m4]]

Both outings were tests, not race weekends. So the World Championship record looks the same as it has since 1976: two women on the grid, one of them with points.[^c1][^c16]
