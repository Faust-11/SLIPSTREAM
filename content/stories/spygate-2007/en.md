## A package from Maranello

The 2007 season should have been remembered for the McLaren–Ferrari duel on track. Instead the year's main battle was fought by lawyers, and it ended with the biggest fine in motor racing history.[^c20]

It started with Nigel Stepney. The Briton had spent years at Ferrari, where he began as chief mechanic.[^c2] Before the 2007 Monaco Grand Prix white powder was found near the fuel tanks of the Ferrari cars, and talk of sabotage began inside the team; Stepney denied everything and said he was being set up.[^c3] In early July Ferrari sacked him.[^c1]

The same week it emerged where Maranello's secrets might have gone. McLaren announced that at the end of April one of its senior technical staff had received a package of technical information from a Ferrari employee, and that the staff member had been suspended.[^c5] He turned out to be Mike Coughlan, McLaren's chief designer, who had once worked with Stepney at Benetton.[^c4]

## The copy shop that broke it open

On 3 July 2007, under the authority of the High Court of England and Wales, Coughlan's home was searched.[^c6] Some 780 pages of confidential Ferrari material were found there.[^c6]

[[media:m2]]

Ferrari was put on the trail by a photocopying shop.[^c7] According to The Independent, the shop was in Woking: Coughlan's wife was copying the documents there, and a member of staff, noticing they were confidential, alerted Ferrari.[^c8] The shop's records indicated the documents had been copied or scanned on or around 2–3 May.[^c9]

On 10 July the case of Ferrari v Coughlan reached the High Court in London. Ferrari's barrister Nigel Tozzi said the Coughlans had "behaved disgracefully by taking these documents."[^c10]

## First verdict: guilty, but unpunished

On 26 July the FIA World Motor Sport Council (WMSC) found McLaren in breach of Article 151c of the International Sporting Code for possessing confidential Ferrari information.[^c11] It imposed no penalty, ruling there was insufficient evidence that the information had been used.[^c11] But the FIA reserved the right to reopen the case. If it turned out that Ferrari's secrets had been put to use, McLaren faced exclusion from the 2007 and 2008 championships.[^c11]

That seemed to be the end of it. The next blow came from inside McLaren.

## A morning in Hungary

In Budapest relations within the team finally cracked. On Saturday Fernando Alonso was stripped of pole and dropped five places for impeding Lewis Hamilton in qualifying.[^c12] On the morning of 5 August, race day, Alonso had a row with McLaren boss Ron Dennis.[^c13]

Accounts of what was said differ. In Dennis's version, an upset Alonso gave no details but mentioned an email from one of the team's engineers; after the conversation Dennis immediately phoned the FIA.[^c13][^c14] British newspapers, citing witnesses, reported something else: Alonso allegedly demanded "Either make me number one or let me go" and threatened to take the Ferrari information to the FIA.[^c14] Alonso's manager Luis Garcia called the reports "complete rubbish."[^c14] Later, according to Dennis, the manager retracted what had been said and apologised, and after the race Alonso apologised to him in person.[^c13]

On 30 August FIA president Max Mosley wrote to the McLaren drivers offering immunity in exchange for evidence.[^c15] The drivers gave evidence, and their correspondence ended up among the material the council examined in September.[^c15]

## What the emails said

On 21 March 2007 McLaren test driver Pedro de la Rosa asked Coughlan for the "Red Car's" weight distribution so he could try it in the simulator, and Coughlan replied by text with Ferrari's figures.[^c32]

On 25 March de la Rosa sent Alonso details of Ferrari's weight distribution, its aerodynamic balance at 250 km/h and the gas Ferrari used to inflate its tyres.[^c16] Alonso replied that testing that gas was "very important".[^c16] De la Rosa named Stepney as the source and called it "very reliable".[^c16] In April de la Rosa also asked Coughlan about Ferrari's braking system.[^c32]

Italian police, meanwhile, had compiled records of the contact between Coughlan and Stepney. According to the council, between 11 March and 3 July 2007 they exchanged at least 288 text messages and 35 phone calls.[^c17]

## 13 September: 100 million

The extraordinary WMSC meeting at the FIA's Paris headquarters lasted all day.[^c18] The ruling was harsh: McLaren were excluded from the 2007 constructors' championship and stripped of all their points for the season; on top of that, the team had to pay a fine of US$100 million, minus the sum Formula One Management would have paid it for its constructors' result.[^c19] Autosport called it by far the largest fine ever imposed in motor racing; Sky Sports called it unprecedented.[^c20]

The drivers were spared. Alonso and Hamilton kept all their points and could keep fighting for the title; the only restriction was that no McLaren representative could appear on the podium.[^c21] In its decision the council wrote that "some degree of sporting advantage was obtained", even if it might never be quantified.[^c22]

Dennis disagreed: "Having been at the hearing today I do not accept that we deserved to be penalised in this way," he said.[^c23] McLaren accepted that Coughlan had held Ferrari documents but insisted the information had not been shared with anyone else or used on its cars.[^c33]

[[media:m3]]

The irony was that four days before the verdict McLaren had scored a one-two at Monza: Alonso first, Hamilton second.[^c24] After that race Hamilton led the standings on 92 points, with Alonso on 89 and Kimi Räikkönen on 74.[^c24]

## No appeal

On 21 September McLaren notified the FIA that it would not appeal, which handed the constructors' title to Ferrari.[^c25] "We believe the time has come to put this huge distraction behind us," Dennis said.[^c25]

The season ended with a twist nobody foresaw. Räikkönen became champion with 110 points, while Hamilton and Alonso scored 109 each.[^c26] Ferrari won the constructors' championship with 204 points, and McLaren appear in the table as excluded, with zero.[^c27]

## An apology and a full stop

The FIA still had to check whether McLaren's 2008 car contained any Ferrari ideas. On 5 December 2007 McLaren Group chief operating officer Martin Whitmarsh wrote to Mosley.[^c28] The team admitted that some Ferrari information may have reached people beyond de la Rosa and Alonso, apologised "unreservedly", and offered to discuss a moratorium on systems linked to that data: quickshift, fast fill and CO2 as a tyre gas.[^c28]

That was enough for the FIA. The council cancelled the hearing scheduled for 14 February 2008, and the case was closed.[^c29] Ferrari accepted the outcome at a sporting level but kept up legal action against individuals in Italy and Britain.[^c30]

The final word came in Italy. In September 2010 the Modena court, sitting in Sassuolo, approved Stepney's plea bargain: a 20-month suspended prison sentence.[^c31] He was found guilty of offences including sabotage and espionage.[^c31]
