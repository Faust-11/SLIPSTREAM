## A season on two fronts

Michael Schumacher began 1994 as if the championship were already settled: he won six of the first seven races and was beaten only in Spain, by Damon Hill.[^c1] By the autumn, though, the season's main events were happening not on track but in FIA offices. Within a few months Benetton went through an investigation into its software, a black flag at Silverstone, a pit-lane fire at Hockenheim and a worn plank under the floor at Spa. In the end Schumacher won the title by a single point.[^c31]

## Menu option number 13

After the San Marino Grand Prix the FIA demanded Benetton's source code for the software that ran the car. The team only handed it over on 18 July, blaming the delay on confidentiality and the need to consult Ford and Cosworth.[^c2] On 26 July the World Motor Sport Council fined Benetton and McLaren $100,000 each precisely for not supplying the code straight away. Because there was no evidence that banned systems had been used in races, no results were changed.[^c3]

Three days later the FIA published the findings of experts from the firm LDRA. Benetton's software contained a launch control system, an automated start. The menu on a connected laptop listed ten options, and launch control was not among them. But scrolling the cursor beyond the tenth option, to option 13, would switch the system on, even though it did not appear on the screen.[^c4] Benetton's explanation that activating it required rewriting and recompiling the code was described in the FIA document as untrue.[^c5]

The conclusion, however, went the team's way. In LDRA's view, the best evidence was that Benetton had not used launch control at Imola. Had the evidence shown otherwise, the council would have been invited to exclude the team from the championship.[^c4] Ten years later, Benetton technical director Ross Brawn still stood his ground: "it was impossible to use Option 13".[^c6]

## The black flag at Silverstone

On 10 July, on the formation lap of the British Grand Prix, Schumacher overtook pole-sitter Hill at least twice. The stewards handed him a 5-second stop-go penalty.[^c7] Benetton were told verbally at 14:27 and officially at 14:31, but Schumacher did not come in. He was then shown the black flag with the number 5 board for three laps in a row. He only served the penalty when the team told him to, having covered at least 14 laps since the notification.[^c8] On the road Schumacher finished second, and the stewards fined Benetton $25,000.[^c9]

On 26 July the World Motor Sport Council took over the case. Schumacher was excluded from the British Grand Prix results and suspended for two races, and Benetton's fine rose to $500,000.[^c10] The organisers were punished too: Clerk of the Course Pierre Aumonier had his super licence suspended for a year.[^c11]

Schumacher appealed. On 30 August the FIA International Court of Appeal in Paris upheld the decision in its entirety. It rejected the argument that the driver had not seen the flag: Schumacher himself admitted seeing the number 5 board, which was shown together with the flag. The court called ignoring the black flag "one of the most serious breaches of the Sporting Code". Because the appeal had suspended the penalty, the ban applied to the two Grands Prix following the court's decision.[^c12] Schumacher missed Italy and Portugal, and Hill won both races.[^c13]

Brawn later said: "the black flag is sacrosanct: even if it was unjustified, we had to respect it."[^c14]

## Fire at Hockenheim

On 31 July, on lap 15 of the German Grand Prix, Jos Verstappen's car was engulfed in flames during refuelling. Several mechanics and the driver himself suffered burns, fortunately minor ones.[^c15] On 10 August the FIA stated that a filter had been removed from Benetton's refuelling rig and that this had caused the fire. The team replied that it had permission and blamed a faulty valve.[^c16] According to the FIA, without the filter fuel flowed more than 12% faster, which in a refuelling race meant a shorter pit stop.[^c17]

[[media:m2]]

Who exactly allowed the filter to be removed was never cleared up. In its statement Benetton claimed it had acted with the permission of FIA technical delegate Charlie Whiting, and Brawn said the same ten years later. But at the World Motor Sport Council hearing the defence, led by barrister George Carman, put forward a different version: the filter had been removed by a junior employee without management's knowledge. The council accepted that version.[^c18] Benetton also denied that the missing filter had caused the fire.[^c34]

The hearing took place on Wednesday 7 September, and Benetton received no penalty.[^c19] FIA president Max Mosley explained that the decision was taken "in the interests of the sport, and in fairness to the other teams who wanted a decision", because otherwise the case would have dragged on until 19 October. Later he admitted: "Maybe we were too easy on Wednesday."[^c20]

## The plank at Spa

From the German Grand Prix onwards every car carried a wooden plank under its floor. If it wore away, that meant the car had been running lower than allowed. Spa was only the third race under the new rule.[^c21]


On 28 August Schumacher crossed the line first in Belgium. The new plank was 10mm thick and had to keep at least 9mm by the end of the race. After the finish, a section about 80cm long on Schumacher's car measured 7.4mm.[^c22] On lap 19 Schumacher had spun and run over a kerb.[^c23] Benetton blamed the wear on that, but the stewards decided its nature and location did not match the marks from the spin.[^c24] Schumacher was disqualified and the win went to Hill. The Briton only learned of it at Brussels airport.[^c25]

Benetton's appeal over Spa was rejected.[^c26] According to Motor Sport, the team left the FIA Court of Appeal on 7 September, and Schumacher's lead over Hill stayed at 21 points instead of 35.[^c27]

## One point

In total the disqualifications cost Schumacher 16 points: six for second place at Silverstone and ten for the win at Spa.[^c28] He missed two more races through the ban.[^c13] After Portugal only one point of his lead remained: 76 to 75.[^c29]

[[media:m4]]

Schumacher returned at Jerez on 16 October and won. Hill answered with victory in Japan, and they arrived at the Adelaide finale at 92 to 91.[^c30] On 13 November Schumacher clipped the wall and then collided with Hill. Both retired after 35 laps, and Schumacher became world champion by a single point.[^c31] The constructors' title, meanwhile, went to Williams: 118 points to Benetton's 103.[^c32]

## What 1994 left behind

No investigation proved that Benetton had raced with banned electronics.[^c4] The team was not punished over the filter.[^c19] The penalties for Silverstone and Spa concerned breaches the FIA considered proven, and appeals changed nothing there.[^c12][^c26] Yet even Brawn admitted that a season of hearings cast a shadow over the title: "I was very frustrated because it tainted a fantastic achievement."[^c33]
