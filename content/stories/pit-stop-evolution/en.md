## 1957: a minute to win back

On 4 August 1957 Juan Manuel Fangio started the German Grand Prix at the Nürburgring with a half-empty tank. Maserati had planned a pit stop for him: refuel mid-race and take on fresh tyres. The Ferraris of Mike Hawthorn and Peter Collins, by contrast, were to run the whole distance without stopping.[^c1] Fangio came into the pits after the 12th of 22 laps with a lead of around half a minute.[^c2]

How long that stop took depends on whom you read. According to Formula 1, the mechanics had managed it in 30 seconds in practice but needed 52 in the race, and Fangio rejoined 51 seconds behind the Ferraris. RaceFans writes of a minute and 16 seconds stationary and a 48-second deficit, Motor Sport that Fangio was close to a minute behind.[^c3] He caught them both anyway and won.[^c4]

Motor Sport historian Doug Nye wrote that from 1961 to 1982 any mid-race pit stop could be expected to ruin a driver's chances of winning.[^c5]

## 1982: Gordon Murray's beer kegs

Brabham changed the game. Its BMW turbo engines were thirsty, so the cars started very heavy. Designer Gordon Murray proposed a different approach: start on half tanks, run faster on softer tyres and refuel mid-race.[^c6] Refuelling during a race had long been allowed, but nobody had yet turned it into a strategy.[^c7]

The equipment had to be invented from scratch. Murray recalled that the team knocked together its first tyre warmers from plywood and built its high-pressure refuelling rigs from old beer kegs.[^c8]

At the 1982 British Grand Prix Brabham unloaded pressurised fuel canisters and fitted its cars with on-board jacks, but the plan was foiled: both cars retired before the scheduled stop. The same happened in France.[^c9] The idea finally worked for the first time on 15 August at the Austrian Grand Prix.[^c10] Race leader Riccardo Patrese came in after 24 laps. He got four new wheels and 24 gallons of fuel in 15.6 seconds and did not lose the lead.[^c11] Patrese never saw the flag, though: his engine later failed.[^c12]

For 1983 Murray built the Brabham BT52 with a smaller tank designed around refuelling. According to RaceFans, he was the only designer to create a car with a half-capacity fuel tank.[^c13] Nelson Piquet became champion in a Brabham.[^c14] From 1984, refuelling was banned and fuel was simultaneously limited to 220 litres per race.[^c15]

Without the hose, the pit stop became a contest in changing tyres. In 1993, the last year without refuelling, Benetton changed Riccardo Patrese's wheels at the Belgian Grand Prix in 3.2 seconds.[^c16]

## 1994–2009: the era of the fuel hose

In October 1993 the FIA World Motor Sport Council announced that refuelling would return for the 1994 season.[^c17] Fire returned to the pit lane with it. At the 1994 German Grand Prix fuel from the hose sprayed over Jos Verstappen's car, it caught fire, and the flames engulfed the driver and the mechanics nearby.[^c18]

[[media:m3]]

On 12 December 2008, meeting in Monaco, the World Motor Sport Council adopted a package of cost-cutting measures that included a ban on refuelling from 2010.[^c19] On 29 April 2009 an extraordinary meeting in Paris confirmed it. The reasoning was practical: teams would save on transporting refuelling equipment, and engine builders would have an incentive to improve fuel economy.[^c20]

## Under two seconds

Once the fuel was gone, only tyres were left at the pit stop, and the seconds began to melt away. The fastest stops are tracked by DHL, which has presented an award for the fastest pit stop since 2015.[^c21]

At the 2013 United States Grand Prix Red Bull turned Mark Webber around in 1.923 seconds. At the 2016 European Grand Prix in Baku Williams changed Felipe Massa's tyres in 1.92 and equalled the record.[^c22]

2019 belonged to Red Bull. At the British Grand Prix it serviced Pierre Gasly in 1.91 seconds, in Germany Max Verstappen in 1.88, and in Brazil Verstappen again, this time in 1.82.[^c23]

On 8 October 2023, at the Qatar Grand Prix, McLaren changed Lando Norris's tyres on lap 27 in 1.80 seconds.[^c24] There were plenty of stops that day: amid fears for the tyres, each set could be used for no more than 18 laps.[^c25] Formula 1 noted that the record was set even though cars and tyres had grown bigger since 2019.[^c26]

As of November 2026, McLaren's record still stands.[^c27] According to DHL, the fastest stop of 2024 took 1.90 seconds, of 2025 1.91 (McLaren, Oscar Piastri at Monza), and the best of 2026 after the Brazilian Grand Prix is 1.99 (Racing Bulls, Arvid Lindblad in Hungary).[^c28] Red Bull won the DHL award every year from 2018 to 2024. In 2025 it went to Ferrari, which set the fastest stop at 10 of the 24 races, and in the 2026 season Mercedes lead after the Brazilian Grand Prix.[^c29]

## Two dozen people

A modern pit stop takes a whole crew. According to Mercedes, up to 22 people are involved, and 12 of them deal with the wheels, three per corner. One loosens and tightens the wheel nut, a second removes the old wheel, a third fits the new one.[^c30] At Williams in 2019, according to Formula 1, the pit crew numbered 23.[^c31] Mercedes performs around 60 practice pit stops over a race weekend.[^c32]

[[media:m4]]

The equipment is not cheap either. According to Formula 1 in 2019, a front jack cost around £250,000, while The Race wrote in 2021 of "up to £200,000".[^c33]

## The price of a mistake

Speed has a downside. At the 2013 German Grand Prix a wheel came off Mark Webber's car after a pit stop and struck FOM cameraman Paul Allen. Red Bull were fined €30,000.[^c34] The FIA responded two days later: during races and qualifying only marshals and team personnel were allowed in the pit lane, mechanics working on a pit stop had to wear head protection, and the race pit lane speed limit was cut from 100 to 80 km/h.[^c35]

At the 2018 Bahrain Grand Prix Kimi Räikkönen was signalled to go before his left-rear wheel had been changed. The car ran over the leg of mechanic Francesco Cigarini, breaking his tibia and fibula. Ferrari were fined €50,000; the stewards wrote that the team "released the car in a manner endangering team personnel and causing injury".[^c36]

In 2021 the FIA turned its attention to automation. The regulations already required sensors at a pit stop to act only passively, and a new technical directive was meant to ensure that no car could leave with an unfastened wheel.[^c37] At first the directive required at least 0.2 seconds between the jack release and the green light for the driver. After arguments with the teams it was softened and postponed from the Hungarian Grand Prix to the 2021 Belgian Grand Prix.[^c38] Red Bull boss Christian Horner was indignant: "Seeing pit stops sub-two-seconds is a remarkable feat and we should be encouraging it, not trying to control it."[^c39]

## The hose may come back

Refuelling during a race is still banned. But in the summer of 2026 FIA president Mohammed Ben Sulayem said its return was being studied, along with the idea of simpler V8s from 2031: "The refuelling we are studying as we speak."[^c40]
