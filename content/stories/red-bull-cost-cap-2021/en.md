In 2021 Formula 1 lived with a spending limit for the first time. In the autumn of 2022 it emerged that the only team to go over it was the one whose driver had won that season's world title.[^c7]

## What the cost cap is

The 2021 spending limit was $145m. The FIA World Motor Sport Council approved the figure on 27 May 2020. The original plan had been $175m, but the COVID-19 pandemic hit the teams' finances and the threshold was lowered.[^c1] It was then due to fall further: $140m in 2022 and $135m for 2023–2025.[^c4]

Not everything counts. Drivers' salaries are outside the cap,[^c2] as are marketing costs and the salaries of a team's three highest-paid executives.[^c3] Teams calculate the rest themselves and submit a report to the FIA, which the Cost Cap Administration then audits. For everyone, 2021 was the first year of the full application of these rules.[^c5]

That same season Max Verstappen won the drivers' title for Red Bull, finishing eight points ahead of Lewis Hamilton: 395.5 to 387.5.[^c6] So when the overspend became known, some rivals went as far as calling for Verstappen to be stripped of the title.[^c35]

## 10 October: a "minor overspend"

On 10 October 2022 the FIA published the results of its review. Seven of the ten teams received certificates of compliance. Aston Martin, the FIA found, had committed a procedural breach. Red Bull had committed both a procedural breach and a minor overspend breach, meaning less than 5%.[^c7] Williams had a separate procedural breach, settled back in May.[^c8] On that occasion the team got away with a $25,000 fine for a late submission.[^c29]

What was Red Bull facing? For a minor overspend the regulations allowed points deductions, suspension from individual sessions other than the race, limits on aerodynamic testing and a lower cap for the following year.[^c28]

Red Bull noted the findings "with surprise and disappointment" and insisted that its submission had been below the cap.[^c9] The FIA did not give the size of the overspend at that point, and until the end of October the paddock could only guess how much money was involved.[^c7]

## Brown's letter

The loudest voice was McLaren chief Zak Brown. On 12 October he wrote to FIA president Mohammed Ben Sulayem that an overspend constituted cheating, because it offered a significant advantage. Brown named no teams.[^c10] In his view, whoever spent more had gained an unfair advantage in developing the car, and that called for a sporting penalty. Among his suggestions for a minor overspend were a 20% cut in wind-tunnel and CFD work and a reduction of the following year's cap by the amount overspent plus an additional fine.[^c11] Brown also sent a copy of the letter to Stefano Domenicali.[^c32]

## 28 October: the agreement

The FIA and Red Bull signed an Accepted Breach Agreement (ABA) on 26 October and announced it on 28 October.[^c12] The public summary of the agreement finally put numbers on the table.

Red Bull had declared £114,293,000 of costs subject to the cap. The auditors found £5,607,000 that the team had wrongly excluded from the calculation or adjusted.[^c13] The document states Red Bull's 2021 cap in pounds: £118,036,000. After the FIA's recalculation the team was over it by £1,864,000, or 1.6%.[^c14] In dollar terms the press reported different figures: AP wrote of $1.8m, Sky Sports of $2.2m.[^c27]

The list of disputed items runs to 13 points. It includes catering, bonuses, the cost of using power units, travel, maintenance and a clerical error in calculating costs recharged by Red Bull Powertrains.[^c15] The FIA dealt separately with the tax question and acknowledged that, had Red Bull correctly applied a notional tax credit of £1,431,348, the overspend would have been just £432,652, or 0.37%.[^c16]

The penalty had three parts. A $7m fine, payable within 30 days. A 10% cut in aerodynamic testing for 12 months, meaning fewer wind-tunnel hours and fewer CFD runs. And Red Bull covers the Cost Cap Administration's costs of preparing the agreement.[^c17]

The document also contains mitigating language. The FIA stated there was no accusation or evidence that Red Bull had acted in bad faith, dishonestly or fraudulently.[^c18] The decision is final and cannot be appealed.[^c19]

## Aston Martin: a fine without an overspend

Aston Martin's case looked quite different. Its agreement was signed on 25 October. The team's spending was within the cap; the problem was the reporting, with 12 items treated incorrectly. They included costs for the new headquarters, the new simulator, additional wind-tunnel fees and catering at the factory. The fine was $450,000.[^c20]

## Two truths

Red Bull team principal Christian Horner spoke sharply but did not rebel:

> "We accept the penalties, begrudgingly, but we accept them."[^c21]

He called the sanctions draconian.[^c22] He saw the sporting part as the most painful: by his estimate, losing 10% of aero testing would be worth between a quarter and half a second a lap.[^c23] Horner explained where the team and the FIA had disagreed: tax, catering and sick pay.[^c24] He went through the sick-pay case separately. Red Bull had treated payments to an ill employee as an excludable cost. "Had the person died, and thankfully they didn't, the cost would have been excluded," Horner said.[^c30] In his words, "not one penny was spent on performance".[^c31] He added that there had been no intent and certainly no cheating, and that some rivals probably owed Red Bull an apology.[^c25] In his view, rivals had pushed for a harsh penalty precisely because it gave them an advantage.[^c33] Not everyone in the paddock sided with Brown: Alpine team boss Otmar Szafnauer said it was time for the accusations to stop.[^c34]

[[media:m4]]

The rivals' case had been set out most clearly by Brown before the verdict: an overspend gives an advantage both in the current car and in developing the next one.[^c11] The FIA's document took neither extreme position. It recorded the overspend, but called it minor and said it had found no ill intent.[^c18]

## What it changed

The first year of the cap produced the first overspend case with real sanctions, and it concerned the champion's team.[^c5][^c6] The episode also showed how complex the accounting behind the round figure of $145m is. Three of the ten teams had procedural breaches,[^c7][^c8] and the FIA itself wrote that the regulations are very complex.[^c5]

The penalty did not hold Red Bull back. The wind-tunnel restriction ran for 12 months,[^c17] and in 2023 Red Bull won 21 of 22 races.[^c26] So the argument over how much 10% of wind-tunnel time is really worth never got a clear answer.
